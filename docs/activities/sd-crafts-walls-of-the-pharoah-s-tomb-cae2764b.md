The Egyptian hieroglyphics alphabet consists of 24
single-consonant signs that function similarly to modern
letters, forming the phonetic basis of the writing
system.
Ancient Egyptian hieroglyphs were a formal writing
system used for over 3,500 years, combining
ideographic, logographic, syllabic, and alphabetic
elements with more than 1,000 distinct
characters. While many hieroglyphs represented entire
words or concepts (ideograms), a subset of 24 symbols
represented single consonant sounds, forming the
Egyptian phonetic alphabet. Vowels were generally not
written, so the alphabet focused on consonant sounds,
similar to early Semitic scripts.
Hieroglyphs could be written in multiple directions: left
to right, right to left, top to bottom, or even in circular
arrangements. The direction was determined by the
orientation of the symbols, particularly those depicting
living creatures, which faced the beginning of the line.
There was no punctuation, and symbols could serve as
ideograms (whole words) or phonograms (sounds or
parts of words), making the system complex to learn
and interpret.
Hieroglyphic writing was primarily used by priests and
scribes, with only about 1% of the population able to
read it. It appeared around 3200 BCE and evolved into
cursive forms like hieratic and later demotic scripts for
practical purposes. The script was considered sacred,
often associated with the god Thoth, and was used
extensively in temples, tombs, and religious texts.
